Atomic Vibrations

An interactive visualization of lattice dynamics: how the springs between atoms turn individual motion into collective waves (phonons), and how that is summarised by the dispersion relation ω(q).

Notes

What am I looking at?

Each dot is an atom, each line a spring. Grey squares at the ends (or around the border in 2D) are held fixed, like rigid walls: waves reflect from them and build standing waves.

Push one atom (Create pulse) and the disturbance spreads as a wave, because every atom feels its neighbours. Any vibration of a crystal can be written as a sum of such waves; their quantised energy packets are phonons.

The dispersion plot (top right) gives the frequency ω of the wave with wavevector q (wavelength λ = 2π/q). Its slope at small q is the speed of sound; its flat top is the highest frequency the lattice can sustain.

Colours: mass m₁, mass m₂ (diatomic), fixed atom.

Key equations

Monatomic chain (one mass m, spacing a):

ω(q) = 2·√(k/m)·|sin(qa/2)| v_sound = a·√(k/m) (q → 0) ω_max = 2·√(k/m) at q = π/a

At q = π/a the wavelength is 2a: neighbours move in opposite directions. Beyond π/a nothing new appears (q and q + 2π/a describe the same motion), so [0, π/a] is the first Brillouin zone.

Diatomic chain (masses m₁, m₂ alternating; spacing between neighbours a, so the unit cell has length 2a and the zone edge is π/2a):

ω² = k(1/m₁+1/m₂) ± k·√[(1/m₁+1/m₂)² − 4·sin²(qa)/(m₁m₂)] v_sound = a·√(2k/(m₁+m₂)) optical ω(q=0) = √(2k(1/m₁+1/m₂)) gap: from √(2k/m_heavy) to √(2k/m_light)

Textbooks often write sin²(q·a_c/2) with a_c = 2a the unit-cell length. It is the same formula.

Acoustic branch (−): neighbouring atoms move together at small q (sound). Optical branch (+): neighbours move against each other, even at q = 0 (in an ionic crystal this oscillating dipole couples to light). No wave can propagate with a frequency inside the gap.

Fixed ends: with N atoms between fixed walls the allowed wavevectors are q = nπ/((N−1)a), n = 1, 2, …

Things to try
  1. Dispersion of a pulse. Press Create pulse in the monatomic chain. The pulse spreads and trails ripples: long wavelengths travel at the speed of sound, short ones slower (the dispersion curve bends over).
  2. Standing waves. Excite n = 1, then the largest n. Compare the periods with the plot and count the nodes. At the largest n, neighbours alternate up and down.
  3. Scaling. Double k: all frequencies grow by √2. Quadruple the mass: they halve. Change a: ω(q) does not change, but the zone edge π/a moves.
  4. Diatomic. Raise m₂ above m₁: the gap opens and the acoustic branch flattens. Excite n = 1 on each branch and see whether neighbours move together or oppositely. Set m₂ = m₁ and the gap closes.
  5. Damping. Set damping to 0 and watch a mode oscillate for a long time; increase it to watch the mode decay.
  6. 2D. Switch to the 2D lattice and create a pulse: it spreads as a circular wave and reflects from the fixed border.
Model assumptions & limitations
  • Harmonic springs: the force is proportional to the displacement from equilibrium. Real crystals are anharmonic (thermal expansion, finite phonon lifetimes); that is not included.
  • Classical atoms. There is no quantisation: "phonon" here means a classical normal mode.
  • Scalar model: each atom moves in x and y with the same stiffness k for both directions. This keeps the maths simple, but real crystals have separate longitudinal and transverse branches with different speeds. The chain is drawn with vertical displacements so that waves are easy to see.
  • Temperature is only the size of the initial random velocities (arbitrary units, equipartition). It is not coupled to a heat bath.
  • Finite size, fixed walls: q takes discrete values. A real crystal has about 10²³ atoms and effectively continuous q.
  • 2D: the dispersion plot shows waves along one lattice row only. The 2D square lattice would need ω(qx, qy) = 2√(k/m)·√(sin²(qxa/2) + sin²(qya/2)).
  • Units of k, m, time and thermal energy are arbitrary. Displacements are in pixels.
